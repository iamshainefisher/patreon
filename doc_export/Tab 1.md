## Post template

Every post uses the same blocks in about 300 words, so each one is quick to write and easy to read.

```markdown
# [Code] [Number]: [Theory point] in E

Teaser: one sentence on what the sound is like. (20 words)

## The idea
What the theory point is, in plain words. (60 words)

## The notes
The scale or formula in E, with scale degrees. (30 words)

## Chord sequence in E
Letters:  E  | A  | B | E
Roman:    I  | IV | V | I

## How it sounds
Why it sounds the way it does, compared with the previous post. (50 words)

## Try it
One thing to play on guitar or keys. (40 words)

## A fifth higher: the same idea in B
The chord sequence in B, with a diagram. (40 words)

Glossary: linked terms. (20 words)
```

A few rules keep the series consistent:

- **Open to all patrons:** the written notes, the chord and scale diagrams, and the glossary links.
- **Tier-exclusive:** the audio, the tab, and any video.
- **Audio:** about 2 minutes, instruments only, with no spoken explanation. Play the sequence in E for about 1 minute 20, then in B for about 40 seconds.
- **Numbers:** letters and Roman numerals in every post; Nashville numbers appear in F4 and ER5, where they are the subject.
- **Eras:** swap "The notes" for "Mode usage", add "Character of the sound" after "How it sounds", and trim "The idea" to stay near 300 words. Add "Listen" with two or three reference songs and their original keys. Every post also carries its diagram sheet and a link to the 10-minute practice routine.

## Post draft: F2, The E major scale

F2 teaches the whole and half step pattern behind every major scale, at about 245 words of public text.

### F2: The E major scale

Seven notes, one pattern of steps, and a sound you already know.

**The idea.** A major scale follows one fixed pattern of whole and half steps: whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half. Start that pattern on any note and you get that note's major scale. Start it on E and you get the scale this series is built on.

**The notes.** E F# G# A B C# D#, then E again an octave higher. E major has four sharps: F#, G#, C# and D#.

| Note | E | F# | G# | A | B | C# | D# |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Degree | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| Step up | whole | whole | half | whole | whole | whole | half |

**How it sounds.** In F1 you played this scale; now listen to where the steps shrink. The two half steps, G# to A and D# to E, give the scale its bright, settled shape. D# sits a half step below the top E, so the 7th note leans upwards and the scale feels finished.

**Try it.** On guitar, play the low E string open, then frets 2 and 4 (E, F#, G#). Move to the A string and play it open, then frets 2, 4, 6 and 7 (A, B, C#, D#, E). On keys, start on E and play up eight notes; the black keys are F#, G#, C# and D#.

**A fifth higher: B major.** B C# D# E F# G# A#. It uses the same step pattern, and only one note differs from E major: A becomes A#.

**Glossary:** major scale, whole step, half step, scale degree, sharp.

## Post draft: F3, Chords in the key of E

F3 introduces triads and Roman numerals, at about 240 words of public text.

### F3: Chords in the key of E

Seven notes, seven chords, and a number for each one.

**The idea.** A triad is three notes stacked in thirds: pick a scale note, skip one, take the next, skip one, take the next. Build one on each note of the E major scale and you get seven chords. Roman numerals name them by scale degree: capitals for major chords, lower case for minor, and a small circle for diminished.

**Chords in E.**

| Degree | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Roman | I | ii | iii | IV | V | vi | vii° |
| Chord | E | F#m | G#m | A | B | C#m | D#dim |
| Notes | E G# B | F# A C# | G# B D# | A C# E | B D# F# | C# E G# | D# F# A |

**How it sounds.** In F2 each note stood alone; here each one carries a chord. I, IV and V are major and sound bright; ii, iii and vi are minor and sound darker; vii° is diminished and sounds tense, so it is rarely used on its own. Every major key follows this same pattern.

**Try it.** On guitar, start with the open E and A chords, then add F#m, G#m, B and C#m as barre chords at your own pace. On keys, play each triad from the table with your right hand, working up from E.

**A fifth higher: B major.** B, C#m, D#m, E, F#, G#m, A#dim (I ii iii IV V vi vii°). The pattern is identical: major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished.

**Glossary:** triad, Roman numerals, major and minor, diminished, scale degree.

## Post draft: F4, Nashville numbers

F4 is the one post that teaches Nashville numbers, at about 205 words of public text.

### F4: Nashville numbers

The same chord chart, readable in any key.

**The idea.** The Nashville number system names each chord by its scale degree, using plain numbers from 1 to 7. Unlike Roman numerals, the number says nothing about major or minor: you add an m, so 6m is the minor chord on the 6th degree (some charts use a dash, as in 6-). Session musicians use it because a chart written in numbers works in any key.

**Chord sequence in E.**

| Letters | E | C#m | A | B |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Roman | I | vi | IV | V |
| Nashville | 1 | 6m | 4 | 5 |

**How it sounds.** In F3 you played all seven chords; this sequence picks four of them. The numbers change how the chords are written, not how they sound: the bright 1, 4 and 5 are joined by the softer 6m, which gives the loop a warm, familiar shape that keeps returning home.

**Try it.** Play E, C#m, A and B for four beats each. Then cover the letters and read only the numbers: 1, 6m, 4, 5.

**A fifth higher: B major.** Same numbers, new letters: B – G#m – E – F# is still 1 – 6m – 4 – 5. This is the real value of the system: change the key and the numbers stay the same.

&#91;image: F4 diagram sheet: Nashville numbers under the chords in E and B\]

**Glossary:** Nashville numbers, scale degree, Roman numerals.

## Post draft: F5, Why a fifth higher?

F5 folds the usual "A fifth higher" block into the post itself, since the fifth higher is the subject, at about 245 words of public text.

### F5: Why a fifth higher?

Why every post ends in B, and how keys are related.

**The idea.** A fifth is the distance from a note up to the fifth note of its scale: from E up to B. Keys a fifth apart are close relatives, because their scales share six of their seven notes. Line the keys up in fifths and they form a loop called the circle of fifths, which explains why the series visits B.

**The notes.** E major: E F# G# A B C# D#. B major: B C# D# E F# G# A#. Only one note changes: A becomes A#. Each step up a fifth adds one sharp, so E has four sharps and B has five.

**Chord sequences in E and B.**

| Key | Chord 1 | Chord 2 | Chord 3 | Chord 4 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| E | E | A | B | E |
| B | B | E | F# | B |
| Roman | I | IV | V | I |

**How it sounds.** In F4 the same numbers worked in any key; now you can hear it. Both keys use the same two major chords, E and B, in swapped roles: B is V in E and I in B, while E is I in E and IV in B. Moving from the E version to the B version feels like a small lift, not a jump, because the scales are so close.

**Try it.** Play E – A – B – E, then B – E – F# – B. The B and F# chords are barre chords on guitar. Listen for how little the sound changes.

### F6: Chords in each mode in E

Seven modes, seven sets of chords, one pattern.

**The idea.** In F3 you built a triad on every note of the E major scale. Do the same with any mode and you get that mode's own seven chords. Every mode in this series starts on E, but each one changes a few notes, so each gets a different set of chords. Think of this post as a map: M1 to M8 take one row at a time.

**The notes.** Every row has three major chords, three minor chords and one diminished chord, in the same order as F3, just started from a different place. Chord 1, the home chord, is major in Lydian, Ionian and Mixolydian, minor in Dorian, Aeolian and Phrygian, and diminished in Locrian.

## Post draft: M1, What is a mode?

M1 opens the Modes sub-series by showing that changing a few notes changes the mood, at about 260 words of public text.

### M1: What is a mode?

Same starting note, different mood.

**The idea.** A mode is a version of the scale with its own pattern of whole and half steps, and so its own mood. Keep the root on E, change a note or two, and the mood changes with it. The simplest example is E major beside E minor: three notes drop by a half step. In modal names, E major is Ionian and E minor is Aeolian; the other five modes follow in the next posts.

**The notes.** G#, C# and D# become G, C and D. Everything else stays the same.

| Degree | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| E major | E | F# | G# | A | B | C# | D# |
| E minor | E | F# | G | A | B | C | D |

**Chords in E.**

| Chord | E | Em |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Notes | E G# B | E G B |
| Roman | I | i |

**How it sounds.** In F5 we moved to a new root; here the root stays on E and the notes move instead. Lowering G# to G is enough to turn a bright chord dark: the 3rd decides whether a chord sounds major or minor, and the lowered 6th and 7th carry that darker colour through the scale.

**Try it.** Play an open E chord, then lift your first finger off the G string to make E minor. Then play the two scales one after the other and listen for where they part.

**A fifth higher: B.** B major: B C# D# E F# G# A#. B minor: B C# D E F# G A. The chords are B (B–D#–F#) then Bm (B–D–F#). The same three notes drop.

## Post draft: M2, Ionian in E

This is the first mode post written to the template, at about 260 words of public text.

### M2: Ionian in E

The plain major scale, and the sound every other mode gets measured against.

**The idea.** Ionian is the major scale under its modal name. Nothing is raised or lowered, so it sounds bright and settled. Every mode in this series changes a note or two of this scale, which makes Ionian the home base we compare them all to.

**The notes.** E F# G# A B C# D# (scale degrees 1 2 3 4 5 6 7). Every step is a whole step except 3 to 4 and 7 to 1, which are half steps.

**Chord sequence in E.**

| Letters | E | A | B | E |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Roman | I | IV | V | I |

**How it sounds.** I, IV and V are the three major chords in the key, so the sequence sounds open and confident. The B chord pulls hardest, because its D# note leans up into E, and landing back on E feels like arriving home. Keep this sound in your ear: each mode to come bends it a little brighter or darker.

**Try it.** On guitar, strum each chord for four beats, using open E and A shapes and a barre chord for B at the 2nd fret. On keys, play the triads E–G#–B, A–C#–E and B–D#–F# over left-hand roots. Listen for the way the B chord pushes towards E.

**A fifth higher: Ionian in B.** B C# D# E F# G# A#. The chords are B – E – F# – B (I – IV – V – I). The pattern is identical; only the starting note moves.

## Post draft: M3, Lydian in E

The template holds for a mode that changes a note, at about 220 words of public text.

### M3: Lydian in E

One raised note makes the major scale float.

**The idea.** Lydian is the major scale with the 4th raised by a half step. In E, that means A becomes A#. This one change removes the downward pull of the 4th, leaving a floating, dreamy sound. Compared with Ionian, only one note has moved.

**The notes.** E F# G# A# B C# D# (scale degrees 1 2 3 #4 5 6 7). The half steps now fall between 4 and 5, and between 7 and 1.

**Chord sequence in E.**

| Letters | E | F# |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Roman | I | II |

**How it sounds.** The F# chord contains A#, the note that makes this mode Lydian, so moving from E to F# puts that bright note in the spotlight. Neither chord pulls hard towards the other, so the loop floats instead of resolving. Compare it with the B chord in Ionian, which pushes you home.

**Try it.** On guitar, strum E for four beats, then play F# as an E-shape barre chord at the 2nd fret. On keys, play E–G#–B, then F#–A#–C#. Listen for the A# in the second chord.

**A fifth higher: Lydian in B.** B C# D# E# F# G# A#. The chords are B – C# (I – II). E# is the same key as F on a piano, but it is spelled E# because the scale needs one of each letter name.

## Post draft: M4, Mixolydian in E

M4 lowers the 7th by a half step to give a relaxed major sound, at about 240 words of public text.

### M4: Mixolydian in E

Major, but relaxed.

**The idea.** Mixolydian is the major scale with the 7th lowered by a half step. In E, D# becomes D. The scale keeps its major 3rd, so it still sounds bright, but the 7th no longer leans up into E, so the music feels settled rather than driven. You hear it in rock, blues and folk.

**The notes.** E F# G# A B C# D (scale degrees 1 2 3 4 5 6 ♭7). The half steps fall between 3 and 4, and between 6 and ♭7.
